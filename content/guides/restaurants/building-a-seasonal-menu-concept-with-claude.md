## What This Guide Covers
- How to use Claude as a **genuine creative collaborator** for new menu ideas, not just a recipe generator
- How to work through **several rounds of feedback** to land on dishes that fit your kitchen, not just dishes that sound good
- How to turn a finished concept into everything you need to launch it, **menu copy, a staff briefing, and a marketing angle**

---

## Why This Matters
Seasonal menu changes keep a restaurant feeling alive and give regulars a reason to come back. But coming up with genuinely good new dishes, ones that fit your kitchen's actual capability, your food cost targets, and your restaurant's identity, takes real creative effort.

**This isn't about learning special commands or tricks.** This is a genuine back-and-forth conversation, you react to ideas, Claude adjusts, and the concept gets sharper with each round rather than arriving fully formed on the first try.

---

## Case Study: Thistle & Thyme
Heading into fall, the owner of Thistle & Thyme wanted to refresh a third of the menu but kept circling the same few overused autumn ingredients, squash, apple, cinnamon, without anything that felt new. Working through several rounds with Claude, describing the kitchen's equipment, the price point, and what had sold well in past seasons, the conversation produced a handful of dish concepts that felt distinct.

**Result:** Three of the five concepts discussed made it onto the actual menu, and one became the season's best-selling new item.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Set the Real Constraints First

```
I want to brainstorm seasonal menu ideas for [restaurant name], a
[cuisine/type] restaurant. Here are my constraints:

Season/theme: [e.g. early fall, harvest-focused]
Kitchen equipment: [what you actually have, e.g. no deep fryer,
limited oven space]
Price point: [target price range per dish]
Ingredients I want to feature or already have access to: [list]
Anything we should avoid: [allergens, overused ingredients,
techniques that don't fit our kitchen]
```

### Step 2: Ask for a Range, Not One Answer

```
Give me five distinct dish concepts based on those constraints. For
each one, include the core ingredients, a one-line description of
how it would be prepared, and why it fits the season.
```

### Step 3: React Honestly and Push Back
If something's close but not quite right, say so directly. "None of these feel distinct enough from what we already serve. Push further from our existing menu."

### Step 4: Stress-Test the Front-Runner

```
Let's stress-test [dish name]. What could go wrong with this dish
during a busy service? Where might food cost run higher than
expected? What's the most likely reason a customer might send
this back?
```

### Step 5: Write the Menu Description

```
Write a menu description for [final dish name and details]. Keep
it under 25 words, make it sound appetizing without overselling,
and match this brand voice: [paste your brand voice guide].
```

### Step 6: Build the Staff Briefing and Marketing Angle

```
Now write two things: a short staff briefing explaining the dish,
its ingredients, and how to describe it to a curious customer, and
a one-paragraph idea for how we could announce this dish on social
media when it launches.
```

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What if none of the five initial ideas feel right?**
Say so plainly and ask for five more, ideally pointing at what specifically felt off, too safe, too complicated, too similar to what you already serve.

**Q: How do I make sure a dish concept actually fits my food cost targets?**
Ask directly: "Estimate a rough food cost percentage for this dish based on the ingredients listed, assuming [your target price]." This won't replace an actual cost-out, but it's a fast first filter.

**Q: Can I use this same approach for a one-night special instead of a full seasonal refresh?**
Yes. Just narrow the scope in your first prompt to one dish instead of five.

**Q: My kitchen staff has opinions too. Should I include them in this process?**
Definitely. Bring the shortlisted concepts to your kitchen team and get their reaction before finalizing, then bring that feedback back into the conversation.

**Q: How far in advance should I start this process before a seasonal launch?**
Three to four weeks is a reasonable minimum.
