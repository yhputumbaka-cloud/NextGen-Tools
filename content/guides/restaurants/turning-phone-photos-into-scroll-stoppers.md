## Why This Matters
Food is one of the most visual categories on social media, and the gap between a blurry, dim photo and a sharp, well-lit one is the single biggest lever on whether a post gets seen. The problem usually isn't taste, most restaurant owners genuinely know what looks good. The problem is time, and not knowing exactly what to fix.

This used to require multiple tools: one app to retouch, a separate app to write captions, a separate mental effort to figure out what's wrong with the shot in the first place. Claude can now do all three in one place. *That collapses three tools into one conversation.*

---

## Case Study: The Copper Pot
The kitchen at The Copper Pot was turning out strong plates, but the Instagram photos looked muddy and rushed, shot quickly under the kitchen's overhead lighting. The owner started uploading photos straight into a conversation with Claude before posting, asking what specifically was hurting each shot and how to fix it next time.

**Result:** Within a month, saves and shares on posts had climbed noticeably, and a regular customer asked if they'd hired a photographer.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Get the Raw Photo Right First
No amount of AI feedback can fix a photo that's fundamentally unworkable. Before anything else, make two changes to how you shoot:
- Shoot near a window or natural light source, turn off the harsh overhead kitchen lights when you can
- Shoot from directly above or at a 45-degree angle, not straight on at eye level, which flattens the dish and hides texture

### Step 2: Upload the Photo and Ask for a Real Critique

```
Here's a photo I took of [dish name] for Instagram. Be honest with
me, what's working, what isn't, and what specifically would make
this a stronger photo? Focus on lighting, framing, and composition.
I'm shooting on a phone, so keep your suggestions realistic for that.
```

This is the step the old "editing app" approach skipped entirely. Claude can see the actual photo and respond to exactly what's in front of it, not generic photography advice.

### Step 3: Iterate Before You Reshoot
If Claude flags something fixable in the same shot, ask for exact cropping guidance. If the fix requires a reshoot, different light, different angle, note it down and apply it next service.

*You're not trying to make today's photo perfect. You're building a feedback loop that makes next week's photos better than this week's.*

### Step 4: Write the Caption Once the Photo Is Settled

```
Now write three Instagram captions for this photo. Our brand voice
is [your three words, e.g. warm, unpretentious, neighborhood-focused].
The goal of this post is [e.g. drive weekend reservations / introduce
a new menu item / build personality]. One caption under 50 words,
one medium length, one longer with a small story or hook. End each
with a call to action and leave space for hashtags.
```

### Step 5: Adapt for Other Platforms in the Same Thread

```
Rewrite this caption for [platform]. Keep the core message the same,
but adjust tone and length to fit, more conversational for Facebook,
more informational for Google. Remove hashtags for Facebook and Google.
```

One photo, one conversation, every platform covered in a few minutes.

### Step 6: Build a Weekly Rhythm
Rather than editing and writing one post at a time, batch this once a week:
- After each service, save your 3 to 5 best phone photos
- Pick one day to run all of them through Claude in one sitting
- Walk away with a week of polished, ready-to-post content

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can Claude actually edit the photo itself, or just tell me what to fix?**
Claude can look at the photo and tell you specifically what's working and what to change, but it doesn't edit the image file directly. Think of it as a sharp second pair of eyes before you post, not a retouching tool.

**Q: My photos still look dark even after I follow the feedback. What's going on?**
The issue is almost always the original lighting, not anything after the fact. A small portable LED panel, available for under $30, placed near the dish during photos solves this more reliably than any amount of feedback on an already-dim shot.

**Q: Can I do this with old photos already on my Instagram?**
Yes. Download any existing post, upload it into a fresh conversation, and ask the same critique question. This is a fast way to learn your own patterns.

**Q: How long should each caption be?**
Vary it. A short caption works for a strong standalone photo. A longer one with a story or hook works when you want the post to build a connection, not just show food.

**Q: What if I want video, not just photos?**
The same approach works, describe what's in the clip or upload a frame from it, and ask Claude for caption and posting guidance the same way.
