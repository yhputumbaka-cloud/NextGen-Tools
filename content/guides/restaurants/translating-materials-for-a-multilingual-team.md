## What This Guide Covers
- How to translate **SOPs, checklists, and onboarding documents** accurately, without losing tone or meaning
- How to handle **restaurant-specific terms** that don't translate literally, station names, dish names, kitchen slang
- How to keep translated materials **up to date** as the original English versions change

---

## Why This Matters
Many independent restaurant kitchens run on a genuinely multilingual team, and written materials often exist only in English. A staff member who's a confident, capable cook can still miss something safety-critical if the only written reference is in a language they're still learning.

---

## Case Study: Casa Del Sol
Casa Del Sol's kitchen was staffed primarily by Spanish-speaking cooks, but every SOP, checklist, and onboarding document existed only in English. The owner used Claude to translate the full set of documents into Spanish, checking each one with a bilingual staff member.

**Result:** New hires started referencing the Spanish materials directly during onboarding, and a few small procedural inconsistencies that had existed for years finally surfaced and got corrected.

---

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Identify Which Documents Actually Need Translation First
Start with whatever's most safety-critical or most frequently referenced.

### Step 2: Translate with Context, Not Just Language

```
Translate this document from English to [target language] for use
in a restaurant kitchen. The tone should be clear and direct, the
way a manager would actually speak to staff, not overly formal.
Here's the document: [paste the document]
```

### Step 3: Flag Restaurant-Specific Terms Up Front

```
Before translating, here are a few terms specific to our restaurant
that I want kept consistent: [list any station names, dish names,
or internal terms, and how you want each one handled].
```

### Step 4: Have a Bilingual Team Member Review It

> This step cannot be skipped. A fluent speaker on your actual team should review it for anything that reads awkwardly or doesn't match how your staff naturally talks.

### Step 5: Keep the English and Translated Versions Side by Side
This helps during training and makes future updates easier to manage.

### Step 6: Update the Translation Whenever the Original Changes

```
Here is an updated version of [document name] in English: [paste
updated document]. Here is the previous translated version: [paste
old translation]. Update the translation to match the changes,
keeping the same terms and tone we established before.
```

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What if my team speaks more than one language besides English?**
Run this same process separately for each language needed.

**Q: How accurate is this compared to a professional human translator?**
For internal operational documents, this is generally strong, especially with a bilingual staff review built in.

**Q: Should training itself happen in the translated language, or just the written materials?**
That depends on your team and your trainers.

**Q: What if a staff member points out the translation sounds slightly off after we've already started using it?**
Take that feedback seriously and update it.

**Q: Can this help with verbal communication too, not just written documents?**
Indirectly, Claude can help prepare key phrases, but real-time verbal translation during a live shift is outside what this guide covers.
