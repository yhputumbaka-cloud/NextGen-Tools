- How to organize a simple portfolio that **shows your best work** without overwhelming a potential client

## Why This Matters
In personal care, people aren't just choosing a business, they're choosing a person to trust with their hair, skin, or body. Your bio is often what tips someone from browsing to booking, especially if you're independent and don't have a bigger brand name behind you.

Most bios end up sounding interchangeable because of default, safe language:
- "Passionate about making clients feel beautiful"
- "Client centered approach"
- "Treats every client like family"

None of that tells anyone anything specific. What actually builds trust is detail: your training, your approach, the kind of client you work best with.

## Step-by-Step Guide

### Step 1: Gather the raw material first
Before writing anything, jot down:
- Your training and certifications
- How many years you've been practicing
- What you specialize in
- Anything about your approach that feels true to how you actually work

Don't worry about phrasing yet. Just get the facts down.

### Step 2: Draft a long form bio for your website

```
Here are the facts about my background:
[paste your notes: training, years of experience, specialties, approach]

Write a website bio, three to five sentences, in first person. 
Make it sound confident and specific, not templated. Avoid phrases 
like "passion for beauty," "client centered," or "treat every 
client like family." Focus on concrete details.
```

### Step 3: Shorten it for Instagram and booking platforms
Ask Claude to adapt the long version rather than starting from scratch.

```
Take this bio and shorten it to under 150 characters for an 
Instagram bio, keeping the most distinctive detail. Then write a 
separate two sentence version for a booking platform profile.

[paste your long form bio]
```

### Step 4: Choose your portfolio photos with intention
A portfolio works best when it shows range within your specialty, not every photo you've ever taken.
- Pick a small number of your strongest, most representative results
- Aim for consistent lighting and framing so the set looks cohesive

*Tip:* Upload a handful of candidate photos directly to Claude and ask which ones look most consistent in lighting and composition as a set. It can spot inconsistencies you might miss scrolling through your camera roll one at a time.

### Step 5: Write short captions for each portfolio piece

```
Here is a description of what I did in this photo: [describe the 
work, e.g. color correction from box dye to natural blonde]

Write a short, specific caption, under 20 words, that a potential 
client would understand even without styling terminology.
```

### Step 6: Review and update every six months
Set a reminder to revisit your bio twice a year as your specialties and experience grow, rather than letting it go stale.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Should my bio mention my years of experience even if it's a small number?**
Yes, but frame it honestly. A newer practitioner can lean on recent, focused training or a specific technique mastered, rather than trying to sound more experienced than they are.

**Q: Can I use the same bio everywhere?**
You can use one core version, but shortening it for platforms with character limits usually reads better than cramming a long bio into a small space.

**Q: What if I don't have professional photos of my work?**
Well lit phone photos taken consistently beat no portfolio at all.

**Q: How personal should my bio get?**
A little personality goes a long way, but keep it focused on your work and approach rather than turning it into a full personal story.
